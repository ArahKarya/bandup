## What the task asks

You get a list of headings (i, ii, iii…) and must choose the best heading for each paragraph.
There are always **more headings than paragraphs**. Matching headings often comes first in a
set, so do it before other question types: understanding the main ideas helps you with
everything else.

A heading is a **summary of the main idea** of the whole paragraph. It is not a description of
one example, one number or one sentence.

## Main idea versus detail

Consider this paragraph:

> "Early clocks were remarkably inaccurate. Some medieval church clocks lost as much as fifteen
> minutes a day, and most had only an hour hand. Owners had to reset them regularly using
> sundials, which themselves were useless on cloudy days."

- **Main idea:** early clocks were not accurate.
- **Details:** fifteen minutes a day, only an hour hand, sundials, cloudy days.

A heading like "The unreliability of early timekeeping" summarises the main idea. A heading like
"The problem of cloudy weather" describes only a detail. It might look attractive because the
word "cloudy" appears, but it is wrong.

## Step-by-step method

1. **Read the headings first** and note the key idea in each. Look for headings that are similar
   and note the difference: "The causes of…" versus "The effects of…".
2. **Do the example**, if there is one, and cross that heading out.
3. **Read one paragraph.** Focus on the first two sentences and the last sentence. Then quickly
   read the middle to confirm.
4. **Summarise the paragraph in your own words** in five words or fewer.
5. **Compare your summary with the headings.** Choose the one that covers the whole paragraph.
6. **If two headings seem possible**, write both lightly and come back after doing other
   paragraphs. Elimination often solves the problem.
7. **Skip difficult paragraphs.** Do the easy ones first; fewer headings remain for the hard
   ones.

## Worked example

Headings:

> i. A surprising source of funding
> ii. Public opposition to a new design
> iii. How the idea spread to other countries
> iv. Engineering problems that delayed construction
> v. The designer's early career

Paragraph:

> "When the plans were published in 1962, many residents were horrified. Letters to local
> newspapers described the structure as 'an ugly concrete box', and a petition against it
> gathered nearly 20,000 signatures. Even some members of the city council, who had approved the
> project, began to express doubts in public."

Summary: people disliked the plans. Answer: **ii**.

Why not iv? The paragraph mentions "concrete", but there is nothing about engineering problems
or delays. Why not v? The designer is never discussed. The letters, the petition and the council
are all examples supporting one idea: opposition.

## Clues inside the paragraph

- **Contrast words** such as "however" or "yet" in the middle can mean the main idea comes
  after them: "Many believed X. However, recent research shows Y." The heading is about Y.
- **Questions** at the start of a paragraph are often answered later in the same paragraph, and
  the answer is the main idea.
- **Summary phrases** like "In short" or "Overall" often introduce the main point at the end.

## Common traps

- **Word matching.** Test writers put paragraph words into wrong headings.
- **Choosing a heading that fits only half the paragraph.** If a paragraph discusses advantages
  and then disadvantages, "The benefits of…" is incomplete.
- **Reading every word of every paragraph.** Read enough to get the gist, then decide.
- **Forgetting the example.** The heading used in the example cannot be used again.

## Practice idea

Take an article from a quality newspaper or magazine. Cover the headline and any subheadings.
For each paragraph, write your own heading in five words or fewer. Then write two "trap"
headings: one that uses a detail from the paragraph and one that uses a word from it but changes
the meaning. Creating traps teaches you to recognise them.
