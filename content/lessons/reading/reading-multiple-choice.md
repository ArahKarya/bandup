## How the task works

You read a question (the **stem**) and choose the best answer from four options (A–D). In
"Choose TWO letters" versions, you choose two answers from five. Questions usually follow the
order of the passage, but the **last question** may ask about the passage as a whole, for
example "What is the best title for the passage?"

Multiple choice tests **careful, detailed reading**. The options are designed so that each wrong
one seems right for a reason.

## Kinds of question

- **Detail:** *According to the writer, what was the main cause of the delay?*
- **Opinion:** *What does the writer think about the new policy?*
- **Purpose:** *Why does the writer mention the 1998 study?*
- **Reference:** *What does "this" refer to in line 12?*
- **Global:** *Which is the best title for Reading Passage 3?*

## How wrong options are made

1. **False:** the passage says the opposite.
2. **Not mentioned:** the option sounds reasonable but the passage says nothing about it.
3. **Partly correct:** half of the option is right, but the other half is wrong. "The project
   was expensive *and unpopular*" when it was only expensive.
4. **True but irrelevant:** it is correct according to the passage but does not answer *this*
   question.
5. **Word match:** it uses the same words as the passage, but with a different meaning.

## Step-by-step method

1. **Read the stem only.** Underline the key words.
2. **Find the relevant part** of the passage by scanning.
3. **Read that part carefully**, usually three to five sentences.
4. **Answer in your own words** before looking at the options.
5. **Now read the options** and find the one that matches your answer.
6. **Eliminate** the others, giving each a reason: F (false), NM (not mentioned), P (partly
   correct), I (irrelevant).

## Worked example

Passage extract:

> "Supporters of the four-day working week often point to higher productivity. In a recent
> trial involving 60 firms, output remained stable while staff reported less stress. However,
> the trial's organisers admit that most participating companies were small and worked in
> office-based sectors. Whether the model could succeed in hospitals or factories, where work
> cannot easily be compressed, remains an open question."

Question: *What does the writer suggest about the trial?*

> A. It proved that a four-day week increases output.
> B. Its results may not apply to all types of work.
> C. Most employees disliked the new schedule.
> D. It was organised by hospitals and factories.

Your own answer before reading the options: *the results might not work everywhere*.

- A: **False.** Output "remained stable"; it did not increase. Also "proved" is too strong.
- B: **Correct.** "Whether the model could succeed in hospitals or factories… remains an open
  question."
- C: **False.** Staff "reported less stress", which suggests a positive reaction.
- D: **Word match.** Hospitals and factories are mentioned, but as places outside the trial.

## Purpose questions

When a question asks *why* the writer mentions something, think of a function verb: to
illustrate, to contrast, to criticise, to support an argument, to give a historical background,
to introduce a new idea. Look at the sentence before and after the mention. Often the example
supports the main claim of the paragraph.

## Common traps

- **Reading the options first** and then looking for one in the passage. This makes every option
  look possible.
- **Extreme words** such as "always", "proved", "all", "never". These are often wrong because
  academic writers usually hedge.
- **Choosing the option with the most passage words.** Check meaning, not vocabulary.
- **Ignoring the stem.** If the question asks about the writer's view, an option that describes a
  researcher's view is wrong.

## Practice idea

Next time you do a BandUp multiple-choice set, label every wrong option with F, NM, P, I or W
(word match). After three sets, count which label appears most often in the options you chose
wrongly. That is your personal trap, and you will start recognising it during the test.
