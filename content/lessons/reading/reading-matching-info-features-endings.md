## Three different tasks

These three tasks all use letters, but they test different skills.

| Task | What you match | Order |
|---|---|---|
| Matching information | A piece of information to the **paragraph** that contains it | Not in order |
| Matching features | Statements to **people, places, dates or theories** | Not in order |
| Matching sentence endings | The first half of a sentence to the correct **second half** | In order |

## 1. Matching information

The question says something like: *Which paragraph contains the following information?*

- 14. a reference to an unexpected side effect
- 15. a comparison between two methods
- 16. an example of a failed experiment

Notice the language: "a reference to", "an example of", "a comparison". These describe the
**type** of information, not a paragraph's main idea. The information may be only one sentence
inside a long paragraph. That is the difference from matching headings.

**Method**

1. Skim the passage first if you have not already. Note the topic of each paragraph.
2. Read all the questions and underline the key concept and the type ("example", "reason",
   "description").
3. Start with questions that contain **scannable words** (numbers, names).
4. For the others, go through the passage paragraph by paragraph, checking all remaining
   questions against each paragraph.
5. Remember that some paragraphs may be used twice and some not at all.

## 2. Matching features

You get a list of people (A Dr Lim, B Professor Hart, C Maria Silva) and a list of statements or
findings. You match each statement to the person who said or found it.

**Method**

1. **Scan for every name** and circle it in the passage. A person may appear in several places,
   sometimes by surname only or as "she" or "the researcher".
2. Read what each person says and make a short note next to the name.
3. Read each statement and look for the paraphrase in your notes.
4. Be careful with sentences where one person **reports or disagrees with** another person's
   view: "Hart disputes Lim's claim that…". The claim belongs to Lim, not Hart.

**Worked example**

> "Dr Lim argues that bilingual children develop better attention control. Professor Hart
> accepts this but warns that the effect may disappear in adulthood. Maria Silva, by contrast,
> found no difference in her study of 400 teenagers."

- *The benefit may not last into later life.* → **B** (Hart)
- *Bilingualism improves focus.* → **A** (Lim)
- *A large study failed to find any advantage.* → **C** (Silva)

## 3. Matching sentence endings

You get sentence beginnings (questions) and a list of endings. You choose the ending that
completes each sentence according to the passage.

**Method**

1. Read the beginnings and underline the key words. Find the right place in the passage (the
   questions follow passage order).
2. **Predict** the ending from the passage before looking at the options.
3. Check the options for **grammar**: does the ending fit the structure of the beginning?
4. Check **meaning**: several endings may be grammatically correct, but only one matches the
   passage.

**Worked example**

> Beginning: *The introduction of electric trams in the city…*
> A. was opposed by most shopkeepers.
> B. reduced the number of horses in the streets.
> C. because of rising fuel prices.
> D. were built by a foreign company.

Grammar removes C (no verb) and D ("were" does not agree with "introduction"). Between A and B,
check the passage:

> "Within a decade of electric trams arriving, horse-drawn transport had almost vanished. Local
> traders, who had feared losing customers, soon found that business improved."

The answer is **B**. A is a trap: traders feared a problem, but the passage does not say they
opposed the trams, and in fact business improved.

## Common traps

- **Treating matching information like headings.** You are looking for one detail, not the main
  idea.
- **Confusing the speaker and the subject** in matching features ("Hart criticises Lim's view").
- **Ignoring grammar** in sentence endings. It can eliminate half the options instantly.
- **Spending too long on one question.** Answer the easy ones first.

## Practice idea

Choose an article that quotes three or more experts. Write each expert's name at the top of a
page and, under each, write their opinion in your own words using no more than eight words.
Then mix up your notes and try to match them back to the names a day later. This builds the
paraphrasing and tracking skills that matching features needs.
