## What IELTS is for

IELTS (International English Language Testing System) measures how well you can use English in
real situations. Universities, employers and immigration offices use it to decide whether your
English is good enough for what you plan to do. Because of this, the test does not reward
memorised tricks for long. It rewards the ability to understand and produce real English under
time pressure. Knowing the format, however, removes surprises and lets you spend your energy on
the language itself.

## Academic or General Training?

There are two versions of the test.

- **Academic** is for people who want to study at university or join a profession that needs
  academic English, such as medicine or nursing in many countries.
- **General Training** is mostly for migration, work experience or study below degree level.

**Listening and Speaking are identical** in both versions. **Reading and Writing are different.**
Academic Reading uses long passages from books, journals and magazines. General Training Reading
starts with short everyday texts (adverts, notices, timetables), then moves to workplace texts,
and ends with one longer general-interest passage. In Writing, Academic Task 1 asks you to
describe a chart, table, process or map, while General Training Task 1 is a letter. Task 2 is an
essay in both versions.

Check which version your university or visa office needs before you book. Taking the wrong one
is an expensive mistake.

## The four sections at a glance

| Section | Time | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| Listening | about 30 minutes | 4 parts, 40 questions. Part 1 and 2 are everyday situations; Part 3 and 4 are academic. |
| Reading | 60 minutes | 3 sections, 40 questions. No extra time to transfer answers. |
| Writing | 60 minutes | Task 1 (at least 150 words, about 20 minutes) and Task 2 (at least 250 words, about 40 minutes). |
| Speaking | 11–14 minutes | A face-to-face interview in 3 parts with an examiner. |

On paper, Listening gives you about 10 minutes at the end to copy answers onto the answer sheet.
On computer, you type answers as you go and get a short check time instead. Reading never gives
extra transfer time, so on paper you must write answers on the sheet within the 60 minutes.

## How bands are calculated

Each section receives a band from 0 to 9, in half-band steps (5.0, 5.5, 6.0 and so on).

**Listening and Reading** are marked right or wrong. Each correct answer is one mark, out of 40.
The raw score is then converted to a band using a table. The exact table can vary slightly from
test to test, but as a rough guide for Listening:

- about 23 correct is band 6.0
- about 30 correct is band 7.0
- about 35 correct is band 8.0

Academic Reading is similar. General Training Reading needs more correct answers for the same
band (around 34 for 7.0), because its texts are easier. There is no penalty for wrong answers,
so **never leave a blank**.

**Writing and Speaking** are marked by trained examiners using four criteria, each worth 25%.
For Writing these are Task Achievement (Task 1) or Task Response (Task 2), Coherence and
Cohesion, Lexical Resource, and Grammatical Range and Accuracy. For Speaking they are Fluency and
Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, and Pronunciation. In Writing, Task 2
counts for about twice as much as Task 1.

## How the overall band is rounded

Your overall band is the average of the four section bands, rounded to the nearest half band.
The rounding rule has one detail that confuses many candidates:

- If the average ends in **.25**, it rounds **up** to the next half band (6.25 becomes 6.5).
- If it ends in **.75**, it rounds **up** to the next whole band (6.75 becomes 7.0).
- Anything below those points rounds down (6.125 becomes 6.0).

### Worked example

Rina scores Listening 7.5, Reading 7.0, Writing 6.0 and Speaking 6.5.

1. Add the bands: 7.5 + 7.0 + 6.0 + 6.5 = 27.0
2. Divide by four: 27.0 ÷ 4 = 6.75
3. Apply the rule: .75 rounds up, so her overall band is **7.0**.

Now imagine her Speaking had been 6.0. The total would be 26.5, the average 6.625, and that rounds
down to 6.5. Half a band in one section changed her overall result. This is why improving your
weakest section is usually the fastest route to a higher overall score.

## Common traps

- **Only checking the overall band.** Many universities ask for a minimum in each section, for
  example "6.5 overall with no band below 6.0". A brilliant Listening score cannot rescue a 5.5 in
  Writing if the rule says no band below 6.0.
- **Ignoring word counts.** Writing below 150 or 250 words is penalised under Task Achievement or
  Task Response.
- **Assuming the computer test is easier.** The questions are the same. Choose the format you are
  more comfortable with, especially for typing versus handwriting.
- **Running out of time in Reading.** Remember there is no transfer time.

## Practice idea

Find the score requirement for the course, job or visa you want. Write it down as four section
targets. Then take one timed Listening or Reading set on BandUp, convert your raw score using the
rough guide above, and note the gap between where you are and where you need to be. That gap is
the starting point for your study plan.
