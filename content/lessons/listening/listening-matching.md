## What the task looks like

You see a numbered list of items (people, places, dates, stages of a project) and a box of
options (opinions, features, reasons). You match each item to one option by writing a letter.
Matching appears most often in **Part 3**, where students and tutors discuss ideas, but it can
also appear in Part 2.

For example:

> What does the speaker say about each of the following tour options?
> **Options:** A. suitable for families B. now cheaper C. needs booking ahead D. has limited
> wheelchair access E. includes a meal F. finishes late
> **Questions:** 21. River cruise 22. Castle walk 23. Food market tour 24. Night bus tour

## Why it is difficult

1. You must hold **two lists** in your head at once.
2. The **questions are in order**, but the **options are not**.
3. The speakers almost never use the exact words in the options. They paraphrase.
4. Several options are mentioned for each item, but only one is the correct match.

## Step-by-step method

1. **Read the instruction.** Can letters be used more than once? How many options are there?
2. **Learn the options first.** Read them two or three times and paraphrase each one in a few
   words. "Needs booking ahead" might be heard as "reserve in advance", "it fills up quickly, so
   call first" or "you can't just turn up".
3. **Note the questions.** These are your "listening signposts". When you hear "the river
   cruise", you know question 21 is starting.
4. **Listen for the item name**, then for the feature that follows it.
5. **Write a provisional letter** and keep listening. The speaker may add a correction.
6. **Cross out options** that have been used (when reuse is not allowed). This makes later
   questions easier.

## Worked example

> "Let's start with the **river cruise**. People often assume it's aimed at families, but
> actually most passengers are couples. What's special about the cruise this
> year is that lunch is served on board, all included. Now, the **castle walk**: it's had a price
> cut since last season, which is great news. Then there's the **food market tour**. That one is
> very small, only eight people, so you really must reserve a place at least a day before. And
> finally the **night bus tour**. Be aware it doesn't get back until after midnight."

Analysis:
- **21 River cruise → E** (includes a meal: "lunch is served… all included"). "Aimed at
  families" sounds like A, but the speaker rejects that idea with "but actually".
- **22 Castle walk → B** (a price cut since last season = now cheaper).
- **23 Food market tour → C** (reserve a place a day before = needs booking ahead).
- **24 Night bus tour → F** (back after midnight = finishes late).
- A and D are not used.

Notice that A was mentioned but rejected, and D was never mentioned at all. Decisions like this
are much easier when you have paraphrased the options in advance.

## Common traps

- **Matching on one word.** Hearing "family" does not automatically mean A. Listen to the whole
  sentence.
- **Losing your place.** If you miss question 22, listen for the name in question 23 and continue.
- **Confusing speakers' opinions.** In Part 3, a matching task might ask for the *tutor's* view.
  Write a small T or S next to notes to track who said what.
- **Forgetting reuse rules.** If letters can be reused, do not cross them out.

## Practice idea

Make your own matching task. Choose five films, books or restaurants you know and write six
short features. Record yourself talking about each one for 20 seconds using paraphrases, not the
exact feature words. Play the recording a few days later and try to match. This builds the
paraphrase awareness that this task tests.
